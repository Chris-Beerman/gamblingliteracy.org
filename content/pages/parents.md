## What your child is probably seeing

You don't have to be worried to start talking. Most young people meet gambling long before they place a bet:

- **In games:** loot boxes, card packs and spin-to-win features, which are paid rewards where you don't know what you'll get.
- **On screens:** betting ads during the games you watch together, and streamers and influencers who gamble on camera.
- **With friends:** pools, friendly bets on games, and fantasy sports. Some older teens bet through a friend's account or on unlicensed offshore sites.
- **At home:** scratch tickets as gifts, family pools for big games.

Most young people who gamble never develop a problem. But some do, and it can start in the teenage years. [Understanding risk](/educators/understanding-risk/) explains who is more likely to run into trouble. The [glossary](/glossary/) explains the words you'll hear, from "parlay" to "prediction market."

## How to talk about it

**Pick an ordinary moment.** A betting ad during a game, a news story, a new game your child mentions. It feels less like a lecture when it's already happening.

**Be curious, not alarmed.** You want to find out what they see and think. Try:

<div class="script" markdown="1">

During a game
:   "That ad just said 'risk-free bet.' What do you think that actually means?"

About games
:   "Does that game have loot boxes or packs? Do you know your odds of getting what you want?"

About friends
:   "Do any of your friends bet on games, or play those apps where you can win money? What do you make of it?"

</div>

**Explain how it works.** The odds are built so the company wins over time. Chance has no memory, so a win is never "due." "Free" bets come with strings attached. The [conversation guide](/educators/conversation-guide/#four-ideas-worth-explaining) explains all four ideas in plain language.

**Be honest about your own gambling.** If you bet on sports or buy lottery tickets, your child knows. Talking about why you keep it small, or why you stopped, teaches more than pretending you don't.

## Keep easy access out of reach

::: research
In a 2026 survey of 1,183 Australian parents, parents making it easier for their teen to gamble, such as placing bets for them, was the strongest predictor of both teen gambling and gambling problems.[^dittman2026] Among the few protective factors studied, parental supervision stands out.[^dowling2017] Both findings show what goes together, not proof of cause.
:::

::: practical
- **Don't place bets or buy scratch tickets for your child.** Many families bet together on big games. What matters is who places the bet, with whose money, and how normal it becomes.
- **Turn on the controls you already have.** [Screen Time and Ask to Buy](https://support.apple.com/en-us/105121) on iPhone and iPad, [Google Family Link](https://families.google/familylink/) on Android, and the family settings on Xbox, PlayStation and Nintendo can require your approval for purchases. Free software such as [BetBlocker](https://betblocker.org/) blocks gambling sites and apps.
- **Watch the money.** Your bank can alert you to gambling transactions on a card your teen uses. Gift cards and payment apps can get around a blocked card.
:::

## If your child is already gambling

Stay calm. You are more useful as someone they can keep talking to than as someone who exploded once.

- **Thank them for telling you.** "I'm glad you told me. I'm not going to freak out."
- **Get the details.** What do they bet on? With whom? Whose money? How do they feel afterwards?
- **Skip the lecture and the threats.** They end the conversation, and your child learns not to bring it up again.
- **Ask about money and mood.** Borrowing, owing friends, selling things, hiding losses, or feeling anxious or low about it are reasons to get more help.
- **If someone is pressuring them over a debt,** treat it as a safety issue first. Involve the school, and the police if anyone is threatened. Don't quietly pay off a debt before you know who it's owed to.

::: clinical "Clinical perspective: what parents often notice"
In the editor's work with parents, families often look back and remember things like these: money their child couldn't explain, or always seemed to need; secrecy about apps or accounts; strong emotion about the scores of games they had no other reason to care about; borrowing from friends or siblings; irritability when a game didn't go their way.

These are examples, not a checklist. Many have ordinary explanations, and none of them diagnoses anything. If several worry you, talk with your child and get help.
:::

## Where to get help

- **In a crisis,** if your child talks about harming themselves: call or text **988**, or call 911 in an emergency.
- **About gambling:** call or text **1-800-MY-RESET** (1-800-697-3738), the free, confidential National Problem Gambling Helpline. Parents can call for themselves.
- **For your child:** start with the school counselor or your child's doctor. **[Teen Line](https://didihirsch.org/teenline/)** lets teens talk with trained teen volunteers in the evenings.
- **For you:** [Gam-Anon](https://www.gam-anon.org/) runs support groups for family members; its hotline is 844-475-4213.

[Get help](/get-help/) has all the numbers, Illinois services, and help for young adults over 18.
