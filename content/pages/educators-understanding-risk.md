## The short version

- **Most young people who gamble do not develop problems.** But a minority do, and gambling problems can start in the teenage years.
- **Risk factors cluster.** The young people at higher risk of later gambling harm are often the same young people at risk of other problems: those who are impulsive, use alcohol or drugs, or are depressed. Boys are at higher risk than girls.
- **Starting and escalating are different questions.** What gets young people to try gambling is mostly social: family, friends, access and attitudes. What turns gambling into harm is more about the individual's mental health and behavior.
- **No checklist predicts a child's future.** Risk factors describe patterns across large groups. Use them to decide where to pay attention and how to offer support, never to label a student.

## How common is youth gambling?

::: research
A 2024 meta-analysis in *The Lancet Public Health* estimated that about **18% of adolescents worldwide** (17.9%) gambled in the past year. There were too few data to estimate how many have gambling problems.[^tran2024]
:::

US data are patchy, because most state school surveys don't ask about gambling. Where they do, the numbers are not small.

::: research
- **Minnesota, 2022:** among 83,545 public school students in grades 8, 9 and 11, **7.9% gambled weekly or more**, and **0.7%** screened positive for problem gambling.[^king2025]
- **Connecticut:** in a survey of 2,425 high-school students, **18.6%** had gambled in the past year.[^zhai2020]
- **Worldwide:** across 44 studies, between **0.2% and 12.3%** of young people met criteria for problem gambling. The range is wide mostly because studies used different screening tools and definitions.[^calado2017]
:::

These figures come from different places, years and definitions. Don't compare them directly or treat any one of them as "the" rate for your school.

## Starting to gamble versus developing problems

Researchers distinguish between factors linked to **participation** (whether a young person gambles at all) and factors linked to **harm** (whether gambling becomes a problem). They overlap, but they are not the same, and they suggest different responses.

### What's linked to starting

::: research
A 2021 review of adolescent gambling found that **gambling by parents, family and friends, and approving attitudes toward it** were consistently linked with young people gambling. Social influences appeared to matter more for participation than psychological ones.[^riley2021] Participation is higher among older children and boys.[^ohid2023]

In a 2026 Australian survey of 1,183 parents, **parents making it easier for their teenager to gamble** (such as placing bets for them, or involving them in the parent's own gambling) was the strongest predictor of both teenage gambling and gambling problems.[^dittman2026]
:::

Most of these studies are cross-sectional snapshots, so they show what goes together, not what causes what. The practical point still holds: young people mostly learn gambling from the people around them, and adults often make the first access easy.

### What's linked to later harm

::: research
A meta-analysis of 15 longitudinal studies, which followed young people over time, found these factors in childhood and adolescence predicted later problem gambling:[^dowling2017]

- **Substance use:** frequent alcohol use, cannabis, tobacco and other drug use
- **Mental health and temperament:** depression, impulsivity, sensation seeking, an undercontrolled temperament
- **Behavior:** antisocial behavior, violence, poor school performance, peers who engage in antisocial behavior
- **Gambling itself:** taking part in more types of gambling, and existing gambling problems
- **Being male**

Most effects were small to medium. A UK government review of 39 reviews had the most confidence in four of these for young people: **impulsivity, substance use, being male and depression**.[^ohid2023]
:::

### Families

::: research
Children who grow up with a parent who has a gambling problem are more likely to develop gambling problems themselves. In one Australian study of 612 adolescents, 23% of those who said their father had a gambling problem were at-risk or problem gamblers, compared with 5% of their peers.[^dowling2017b] The UK review rated its confidence in family influences as very low, or the evidence as insufficient, so the size of the effect is uncertain.[^ohid2023]
:::

::: clinical
Many of the adults Chris Beerman treats for gambling describe a parent or grandparent who gambled, and many parents he works with are worried about passing it on. A family history is a reason to talk about gambling openly and early. It is not a prediction, and it should never be named in front of a student or used to single them out.
:::

## What protects young people

::: research
Fewer studies look at protective factors than at risk factors. Among the few protective factors the longitudinal meta-analysis identified were **parental supervision** and **higher socioeconomic status**.[^dowling2017] Its authors note that protective factors, and factors at the level of schools and communities, are understudied.
:::

::: practical
Things adults can do that fit what is known, though none has been tested specifically as gambling prevention:

- **Stay connected and know what they're doing**, including online. Supervision showed up as protective, and it is something adults can influence.
- **Don't make access easy.** Avoid buying lottery or scratch tickets for young people, placing bets for them, or making family events about betting.
- **Talk about the advertising.** Help young people see how offers and odds are designed.
- **Respond to the bigger picture.** Because gambling risk overlaps with substance use, depression and impulsivity, the support a young person gets for those problems is also relevant to gambling.
:::

## The environment young people are growing up in

Risk isn't only about the individual. Young people today meet gambling and gambling-like products in places earlier generations didn't.

{{ figure digital-advertising | A student looks up at an oversized phone full of betting offers while a teacher points to it. }}

### Advertising

::: research
Across age groups, including studies of young people, more exposure to gambling advertising is associated with more positive attitudes toward gambling, stronger intentions to gamble and more gambling, with some evidence that more exposure goes with more gambling.[^bouguettaya2020] Researchers disagree about how strongly this shows cause. One literature review found no longitudinal studies that could test it,[^newall2019] while an umbrella review concluded the evidence consistently supports a causal relationship, especially for children and young people.[^mcgrane2023] The Lancet Public Health Commission concludes that exposure to gambling advertising and marketing is associated with increased rates of problem gambling.[^wardle2024]
:::

### Loot boxes and gambling-like games

::: research
Across studies, spending on loot boxes (paid, randomized rewards in video games) is consistently linked with problem gambling. A review of 13 publications found a positive relationship in 12 of them.[^spicer2022] A UK survey of 16- to 18-year-olds found a moderate-to-large link.[^zendle2019] What these studies can't yet show is the **direction**: whether loot boxes lead young people toward gambling, or whether young people already drawn to gambling are drawn to loot boxes.
:::

::: open
Does early exposure to gambling-like game features cause later gambling for money? One Belgian study that followed adolescents for a year found gambling-like game elements predicted later gambling, but other explanations remain possible.[^grosemans2025] More long-term studies are needed.
:::

## Signs that more support may be needed

::: clinical
When gambling has started to cause harm, adults tend to notice changes in **money** (unexplained cash, borrowing, debts to friends, selling things), **mood** (irritability, anxiety or low mood tied to results), **secrecy** (hidden apps or accounts, lying about time or money), and **time** (gambling crowding out school, sport or friends).

These are examples drawn from clinical experience, not a screening tool. Many have ordinary explanations.
:::

If you notice several, talk with the young person and with your school counselor. Counselors can use a validated screen; the [conversation guide](/educators/conversation-guide/#for-counselors-social-workers-and-psychologists) names one. The [conversation guide](/educators/conversation-guide/#if-a-young-person-says-they-already-gamble) covers how to raise it, and [Get help](/get-help/) covers where to turn.

## What this means for you

::: advocacy
- **Offer general gambling education to every student.** Don't target only those you think are "at risk." Risk factors don't identify individuals reliably, and singling students out can stigmatize them.
- **Add more support where it's needed.** A student who is already struggling with substance use, depression or impulsivity, or who tells you gambling is causing problems, should get more: a conversation, a counselor, a referral.
- **Include gambling where schools already teach about risk.** Health education on alcohol, drugs, mental health and online safety is the natural home for it.
:::
