## What this is

Gambling Literacy is an education and advocacy initiative for the adults who reach young people: middle- and high-school teachers, school counselors, coaches, youth workers and parents. Its job is to help those adults answer three questions:

1. **Who** may be at greater risk of gambling-related harm?
2. **When** are there useful opportunities to reach them?
3. **What** should we teach, say or do at that point?

Young people are the reason it exists, but they are not its audience. The site gives adults plain explanations, conversation openers, classroom ideas and routes to support, and it argues for schools and communities to treat gambling the way they already treat alcohol, vaping and online safety: as something worth teaching about before it becomes a problem.

It launched in October 2026 as a small site on purpose: [a starting guide for educators](/educators/), [one conversation guide](/educators/conversation-guide/) you can print and use, [a summary of the evidence and an advocacy agenda](/research-and-advocacy/), and [a support page](/get-help/). Larger pieces, such as lesson sequences, staff training and original research, will be added when they are ready and checked, and each will be labelled as new when it arrives.

## Who writes it {#editor}

**Chris Beerman**, LCSW, CADC, is a clinical social worker in Chicago. He has worked in addiction treatment for about eight years, in hospital detox and residential programs and, since 2022, in his own private practice, [Chicago Addiction Therapy](https://www.chicagoaddictiontherapy.com/), where he treats adults with gambling, sports betting, high-risk trading and alcohol problems and works with their partners and families. He holds a Master of Social Work from Loyola University Chicago (2020), is licensed as a clinical social worker in Illinois (LCSW) and Minnesota (LICSW), and is a Certified Alcohol and Drug Counselor (CADC). He also edits the [Gambling Research Index](https://gambling-research.com/about/).

He is not a teacher and does not claim to know a classroom better than the people who work in one. What he brings is the other end of the story. Many of the adults he treats started gambling young, often in ways that looked harmless at the time, and many of the parents he works with say they wish someone had told them what to look for sooner. Gambling Literacy is his attempt to move some of that knowledge upstream, to the adults who see young people every day.

## Why now

Gambling has changed faster than most schools' health curricula. Sports betting is now legal in most US states, and betting ads, odds and "boosts" are woven into the sports young people watch. Gaming has its own chance-based purchases. Prediction markets and social casino apps blur the line further. Young people meet all of this long before they can legally bet, usually without an adult explaining how it works. The [Research and Advocacy](/research-and-advocacy/) page sets out what the evidence does and does not say about this.

## Editorial standards {#standards}

Every page on this site follows these rules.

**Claims are labelled.** Each statement is one of six kinds, and the page says which:

<ul class="label-key">
<li><strong>Research finding</strong><br>what studies show, with the source linked</li>
<li class="k-clinical"><strong>Clinical perspective</strong><br>the editor's experience in practice; not data</li>
<li class="k-advocacy"><strong>Advocacy recommendation</strong><br>what we think schools and communities should do</li>
<li class="k-practical"><strong>Practical suggestion</strong><br>a sensible idea that has not been tested as such</li>
<li class="k-open"><strong>Open question</strong><br>what the research has not answered</li>
<li class="k-proposed"><strong>Proposed</strong><br>work we plan but have not done</li>
</ul>

**Facts link to their source.** Statistics and research findings link to the original study or to an authoritative body (a government agency, a peer-reviewed review, a national council), not to news coverage or another website's summary. Where a source has important limits (a single country, a small sample, a survey that shows association rather than cause), the page says so.

**No overclaiming.** The site does not say that knowing more about gambling is proven to prevent harm, because that has not been shown. It does not claim a single proven "best age" to start, because there isn't one. It does not offer risk scores, and nothing here is a screening or diagnostic tool. Population-level research describes groups; it cannot predict what will happen to any one young person.

**Pages carry a review date.** Every page shows when it was last reviewed and when it will be reviewed again. Helpline numbers and legal ages are rechecked at each review.

**Unverifiable claims stay out.** If a figure cannot be traced to a primary source it is left out, or clearly flagged for review while it is checked.

**How it is written.** Pages are researched and drafted by the editor with AI assistance, then checked by him against the sources before publication. Errors are his, and corrections are welcome.

## Independence and interests

Gambling Literacy takes no funding, advertising, sponsorship or data from gambling operators, sportsbooks, casinos, lotteries, game companies that sell loot boxes, prediction-market exchanges, or organizations they fund. It carries no ads and no tracking, and it collects nothing about visitors. Nothing on the site asks for, stores or transmits information about students.

Interests to declare: the editor runs a private practice that treats gambling problems in adults and may benefit reputationally from this work. He also runs HowToStopBetting.com, a separate project for adults who want to stop sports betting. Neither is promoted here beyond the links above.

The site has no organizational status beyond being Chris Beerman's personal initiative. It is not a nonprofit, not affiliated with any school district, and has no partners at launch. If that changes, this page will say so.
