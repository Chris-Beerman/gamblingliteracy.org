## How to read this page

Research findings summarize published studies and link to them. Advocacy recommendations are our view of what should happen, informed by that research but not proven by it. Anything we plan to do but haven't done is marked **Proposed**. To follow new studies as they come out, see the [Gambling Research Index](https://gambling-research.com/research/topics/youth/), which lists new research on youth and young adults every night.

## Evidence summaries

### How many young people gamble

::: research
- Worldwide, about **18%** of adolescents gambled in the past year (17.9%), and about **10%** gambled online.[^tran2024][^wardle2024]
- In Minnesota's 2022 student survey, **7.9%** of students in grades 8, 9 and 11 gambled weekly or more often.[^king2025]
- In a 2023 NCAA survey of 18- to 22-year-olds, **58%** had done at least one sports betting activity.[^ncaa2023]
:::

**Limitations.** The global estimates combine studies that measured gambling differently, and there were too few studies to estimate how many adolescents have gambling problems worldwide. Most US states don't ask about gambling in their school surveys, so there is no reliable national figure for US teenagers. The NCAA survey used an online panel and was not peer-reviewed.

### What school programs change {#what-programs-change}

::: research
Reviews of school-based gambling education agree on two things.

**Programs improve knowledge and beliefs.** Every study in a 2017 systematic review found effects on knowledge, perceptions or beliefs about gambling.[^keen2017] A 2026 umbrella review of seven reviews found programs "consistently improved knowledge and beliefs."[^cabrera2026]

**Effects on behaviour are much less certain.** In the 2017 review, only 9 of 19 studies measured gambling behaviour, and only 5 of those found a change, with short follow-up.[^keen2017] The 2026 umbrella review described behavioural effects as "modest, inconsistent, and rarely sustained."[^cabrera2026] Studies have found students' knowledge improving without a matching change in how they gamble.[^calado2020]
:::

**Limitations.** The evidence base is small and weak. All seven reviews in the 2026 umbrella review were rated "critically low" quality on a standard appraisal tool, and they overlap heavily in the studies they include.[^cabrera2026] A 2023 review of 32 studies rated only 9 as good quality.[^monreal2023] Few studies follow students for long enough to see whether gambling problems are prevented.

**What this means.** Knowing more about gambling has not been shown to prevent gambling harm on its own. That is not a reason to skip education. Young people deserve accurate information about products marketed to them, and knowledge may be a necessary first step. But it is a reason to pair education with the other things adults can do, and to evaluate what schools try.

### Advertising

::: research
Studies consistently find that young people who see more gambling advertising have more positive attitudes towards gambling, stronger intentions to gamble and more gambling, with some evidence of a dose-response relationship.[^bouguettaya2020]
:::

**Limitations.** Most studies are cross-sectional. Researchers disagree about whether the evidence shows advertising causes these effects: one review found no longitudinal studies that could test it,[^newall2019] while an umbrella review judged the evidence consistently supportive of a causal link, especially for young people.[^mcgrane2023]

### Loot boxes

::: research
Spending on loot boxes in video games is consistently associated with problem gambling: 12 of 13 publications in one review found a positive link.[^spicer2022]
:::

**Limitations.** Nearly all studies are cross-sectional, so the direction is unknown: loot boxes may lead young people towards gambling, or young people inclined to gamble may be drawn to loot boxes.

### Who is at greater risk

The research on risk and protective factors is summarized on [Understanding risk](/educators/understanding-risk/). In brief: impulsivity, substance use, depression and being male are the best-supported risk factors for later gambling harm, while parental supervision is one of the few protective factors studied.[^ohid2023][^dowling2017]

## Our research questions {#research-questions}

These are the questions Gambling Literacy wants to help answer. Some will be answered by others' research, which the [Research Index](https://gambling-research.com/) will track; some may become our own projects.

::: open
1. **Timing.** Is there an age, grade or transition at which gambling education changes behaviour, not just knowledge?
2. **Dose.** Do short, repeated conversations across several years work better than a single lesson?
3. **Messenger.** Are coaches, teachers, counselors or parents more effective at reaching different young people?
4. **New products.** How are young people encountering prediction markets, sweepstakes casinos and gambling-like game features, and how should education respond?
5. **US data.** How many US teenagers gamble, on what, and with what harm? Most states don't ask.
:::

::: proposed "Proposed: work we plan but have not started"
- **A survey of educators** about what they see, what they teach and what they need. Proposed; not yet designed or approved.
- **A short evaluation of the conversation guide** with schools that use it, measuring what changes for students. Proposed; would require school partners and ethical review.
- **An expanded resource library**, including lesson plans for health and math classes and a staff training session. Proposed.

No partners, funders or data collection are in place for any of these. When they are, this page will say so.
:::

## Priorities for schools {#for-schools}

::: advocacy
1. **Put gambling in the health curriculum,** alongside alcohol, drugs, vaping and online safety, starting in middle school and continuing through high school.
2. **Teach chance in math class.** Probability lessons are a natural, low-stakes place to explain house edges and why "due" wins aren't real.
3. **Train the people students already talk to.** Counselors, coaches and advisors should know the basics, the warning signs and where to refer.
4. **Include gambling in student support.** Ask about it where schools already ask about substance use and mental health, and know where to refer.
5. **Set clear policies on betting in school sports,** including betting on games by students, staff and families.
6. **Keep gambling out of fundraising,** such as raffles and casino nights aimed at students.
7. **Tell families.** Share basic information and helpline numbers in the places schools already reach parents.
8. **Evaluate what you try.** Given how thin the evidence is, schools that measure the effect of their programs help everyone.
:::

## Priorities for communities and policymakers

::: advocacy
- **Ask about gambling in state school surveys,** so communities can see what young people are actually doing.
- **Limit the gambling advertising young people see,** especially during sports broadcasts and on the platforms they use.
- **Fund prevention, not only treatment,** including school-based education and its evaluation.
- **Treat gambling-like features in games as a youth protection issue,** with clear odds disclosure and age limits.
:::

To follow gambling bills and court decisions across the US, see the Research Index's [bill tracker](https://gambling-research.com/bills/).
